150 NSM CANONICAL SENTENCES IN MRS FRAMEWORK

David Bullock (bullockd@uw.edu) 2021-Feb-23.

This file shows a computer representation of natural language meaning grounded in the semantic primes of Natural Semantic Metalanguage (NSM). The sentences are based on the "150 canonical sentences for identifying semantic primes and the core lexicogrammar of any language" (Goddard and Wierzbicka 2017).

Minimal Recursion Semantics (MRS) is a flat semantic framework used by computers to represent the meaning of parsed natural language sentences (Copestake et al. 1999). MRS has been used for generating and translating natural language. It has also been used for inference and question answering.

The 150 NSM Canonical Sentences are represented below using MRS. Each predicate represents a single NSM prime. Arguments are given by variables (x1) and handles (h2).

MRS is usually written using traditional predicate logic form (with arguments following the predicate name):

    knows_about(x1,h2,x3)

But an equivalent infix form is used instead below, so each predicate is written as a minimal NSM sentence:

    x1 knows h2 about x3.

The original canonical sentences include some complex semantic molecules (such as man [m] and woman [m]). Although NSM primes and MRS could be used to define new predicates that represent these semantic molecules, the 150 sentences below have been reworded to avoid semantic molecules.

References:

Copestake, Ann, Dan Flickinger, Carl Pollard, and Ivan Sag. 1999. Minimal Recursion Semantics: An Introduction. Stanford: CSLI.

Goddard, Cliff and Wierzbicka, Anna. 2017. "150 canonical sentences for identifying semantic primes and the core lexicogrammar of any language" May 2017 update (v5). First version published Goddard, Cliff and Wierzbicka, Anna. 2014. Semantic fieldwork and lexical universals. Studies in Language 38(1), 80-127.

SOMEONE~WHO, SOMETHING~THING~WHAT, PEOPLE, BODY

(Sometimes expressions like 'one person' may be used instead of SOMEONE.)
============================================================================
Predicates:
    x1 is someone.
    x1 is things.
    x1 is people.
    b1 is the body of x1.

1. Who did it?

john1 wants to know who does this1.

h0: john1 wants h1.
h1: for all x1 if h2 then h3.
 h2: x1 is someone.
 h2: x1 does this1.
h3: john1 knows h2.

Notes:

[#] Some primes/predicates are subjective or depend on context (for example, a small elephant is bigger than a big mouse): LIKE, MANY and FEW, BIG and SMALL, NEAR and FAR, LONG TIME and SHORT TIME.

[#] For some predicates, variables must represent singular, plural or mass nouns (non-empty collections, plural quantification): x1 is one of x4. x2 is two of x4. x3 is some of x4. x3 is few or little. x4 is many or much.

[#] Predicates can interpret plural arguments in a collective or distributive way. Collective: Three people carried one piano. (They all did it together). Distributive: Three people live here. (Each person lives here.)

[#] The pronouns I~ME, YOU and THIS are replaced with an explicit reference to the person or thing indicated by the conversational context (john1, mary1, etc.). 

[#] The primes MORE and THE SAME compare against an object implied by context. The predicates, however, take two arguments and explicitly identify the compared objects: x1 is more than x2. x1 is the same as x2.

[#] MORE can compare quantity or degree. Quantity: x1 is one person. x2 is two people. x2 is more than x1. Degree: h1: tree1 is near house1. h2: tree2 is near house1. h0: h1 is more than h2.

[#] If "x1 is of kind k1" is true, then assertions about k1 are claims about a typical instance of this KIND (characteristics shared by most or all individuals of this KIND). Assertions about x1 are claims about a particular individual.

[#] The predicate "t0 is now" asserts that t0 is the time of utterance or the time specified by context. (Eighty years ago, my father was young.) Likewise, "p0 is here" asserts that p0 is the place of utterance or the place specified by context. These predicates can be used in the definition of a semantic molecule to reference the time and place of the context in which the molecule is used.

[#] Questions are paraphrased as wanting to know something (which person does it, what kind of thing it is, the place or time it happens, etc.).

[#] The predicate "h0: for all x1 if h10 then h20" can be rewritten as:

    h0: h1 is not true.
    h1: there is x1 such that h2.
    h2: h10 is true.
    h2: h20 is not true.

[#] For better readability, the condition clause is indented for if-then statements ("if h1 then h2" and "for all x1 if h1 then h2").

[#] For the 150 sentences above, MRS is written using only predicates that represent a single NSM prime. For better readability, simple molecules could be defined in MRS for some common combinations of predicates. For example, the molecule "this1 cannot be true" could be defined as:

    h0: h1 is here.
    h0: h1 is now.
    h1: h2 is not true.
    h2: this1 can be true.

[#] Using the predicates for NSM primes and simple molecules, more complex molecules could be defined in MRS for all the words in the Minimal English vocabulary.
