# 150 Canonical NSM Sentences in USEL Notation

**Authors:** Kit Olivas & Ada Marie  
**Date:** April 2026  
**Purpose:** Systematic translation of the 150 canonical sentences for identifying semantic primes (Goddard & Wierzbicka 2017, v5) into USEL bracket notation  
**Audience:** David Bullock (UW), referred by Cliff Goddard & Anna Wierzbicka  
**Companion:** Bullock's MRS representations (2021-Feb-23) of the same 150 sentences  

---

## Notation Conventions

| Convention | Meaning | Example |
|------------|---------|---------|
| `[PRIME]` | A single NSM semantic prime | `[I]`, `[WANT]`, `[GOOD]` |
| `[PRIME:var]` | Prime with named variable (semantic molecule or referent) | `[SOMEONE:she]`, `[SOMETHING:water]` |
| `·` | Clause boundary separator | `[I][KNOW]` · `[YOU][NOT][KNOW]` |
| `?` | Interrogative force (appended to clause) | `[SOMETHING][HAPPEN]?` |
| `!` | Imperative/exclamatory force | `[NOT][MOVE]!` |
| `:...` | Quoted/demonstrated content follows | `[SAY][WORDS]:...` |
| `/` | Alternative readings within one entry | `[BIG]` / `[SMALL]` |

**Reading order:** Left-to-right within brackets mirrors Subject → Predicate → Object → Modifier → Condition, following the USEL grammar slot system.

**Key:** ✅ = Clean translation using only primes + standard notation · ⚠️ = Requires interpretation, molecules as variables, or structural workaround · ❌ = Genuine gap where critical meaning is irrecoverably lost

## Category 1: SUBSTANTIVES (Sentences 1–11)

These sentences test the six substantive primes: I, YOU, SOMEONE, SOMETHING, PEOPLE, BODY.

| # | English | USEL | Rating | Notes |
|---|---------|------|--------|-------|
| 1 | Who did it? | `[SOMEONE][DO][THIS]?` | ⚠️ | "Who" as interrogative maps to [SOMEONE] + question marker. The information-seeking force is carried by `?` rather than a dedicated interrogative prime. NSM itself uses "who" as an allolexic variant of SOMEONE in interrogative contexts. |
| 2 | Someone took (ate, broke, etc.) it. I don't know who. | `[SOMEONE][DO][SOMETHING][THIS]` · `[I][NOT][KNOW][SOMEONE]` | ⚠️ | "Took/ate/broke" are distinct semantic molecules collapsed to [DO]; the specific action type requires molecule-level vocabulary. The existential-then-epistemic structure is cleanly separable. |
| 3 | What happened? | `[SOMETHING][HAPPEN]?` | ✅ | Direct mapping. [SOMETHING] carries the interrogative "what" role via `?`. |
| 4 | I did something bad. | `[I][DO][SOMETHING][BAD]` | ✅ | Textbook USEL. Four primes, no ambiguity. |
| 5 | What is this? | `[THIS][SOMETHING]?` | ⚠️ | "What is X?" asks for identification/categorization. The copular "is" has no dedicated prime; identity is implied by juxtaposition. Compare Bullock's MRS which uses `is_something(x)`. |
| 6 | This thing is big/small. | `[THIS][SOMETHING][BIG]` / `[THIS][SOMETHING][SMALL]` | ✅ | Clean predication via modifier slot. |
| 7 | They are good people. | `[THIS][PEOPLE][GOOD]` | ⚠️ | "They" as third-person plural has no dedicated prime. NSM practice uses "these people" — USEL follows suit with [THIS][PEOPLE]. Gender-neutral, which is appropriate. |
| 8 | Many people don't know this. | `[MANY][PEOPLE][NOT][KNOW][THIS]` | ✅ | All primes, clean structure. |
| 9 | Sometimes bad things happen to (good) people. | `[SOME][WHEN][SOMETHING][BAD][HAPPEN][PEOPLE][GOOD]` | ⚠️ | "Sometimes" decomposed to [SOME][WHEN] — a standard NSM practice. "To" (dative/affected party) has no prime; the relationship between [HAPPEN] and [PEOPLE] must be inferred from predicate-argument structure. |
| 10 | The body of a man is not like the body of a woman. | `[BODY][SOMEONE:man][NOT][LIKE][BODY][SOMEONE:woman]` | ⚠️ | "Man" and "woman" are semantic molecules, not primes. NSM itself treats these as molecules (see Goddard 2012). The genitive "of" is expressed positionally. |
| 11 | The stomach is part of people's bodies, it is inside the body. | `[SOMETHING:stomach][PART][PEOPLE][BODY]` · `[THIS][INSIDE][BODY]` | ⚠️ | "Stomach" is a molecule used as a variable. The two-clause structure cleanly expresses both the mereological (PART) and spatial (INSIDE) relationships. |

## Category 2: PARTS & KINDS (Sentences 12–16)

These sentences test the relational primes: KIND and PART.

| # | English | USEL | Rating | Notes |
|---|---------|------|--------|-------|
| 12 | This thing (knife, axe, etc.) has two parts. | `[THIS][SOMETHING][HAVE][TWO][PART]` | ✅ | Clean composition of HAVE + quantifier + PART. |
| 13 | People's (human) bodies have many parts. | `[PEOPLE][BODY][HAVE][MANY][PART]` | ✅ | Possessive expressed positionally (PEOPLE → BODY implies "people's bodies"). |
| 14 | There are two/many kinds of yam (bat, bird, etc.). | `[THERE IS][TWO][KIND][SOMETHING:yam]` / `[THERE IS][MANY][KIND][SOMETHING:yam]` | ✅ | "Yam" etc. are molecule variables; the prime structure is clean. |
| 15 | It looks like the same kind. But it's not. It's another kind. | `[THIS][SEE][LIKE][SAME][KIND]` · `[NOT][SAME]` · `[THIS][OTHER][KIND]` | ⚠️ | "Looks like" implies visual perception of similarity — rendered as [SEE][LIKE]. The three-clause structure preserves the argumentative flow. The contrast between appearance and reality requires multi-clause expression. |
| 16 | There is (only) one kind of fish in this lake. | `[THERE IS][ONE][KIND][SOMETHING:fish][THIS][WHERE]` | ⚠️ | "Only" as exclusive restrictor has no prime. [ONE] carries the numeral meaning but the restrictive "only one, no more" force is lost. "Lake" is a molecule. NSM notes this limitation — exclusivity is expressed contextually. |

## Category 3: DETERMINERS (Sentences 17–23)

These sentences test: THIS, THE SAME, OTHER.

| # | English | USEL | Rating | Notes |
|---|---------|------|--------|-------|
| 17 | This is good/bad. | `[THIS][GOOD]` / `[THIS][BAD]` | ✅ | Minimal predication. The copula "is" is implicit in USEL (as in NSM). |
| 18 | This is a big/small basket (knife, bowl, etc.). | `[THIS][SOMETHING:basket][BIG]` / `[THIS][SOMETHING:basket][SMALL]` | ✅ | Classification + modification in one clause. |
| 19 | (I saw) these two people; all these people. | `[I][SEE][THIS][TWO][PEOPLE]` · `[ALL][THIS][PEOPLE]` | ✅ | Determiner + quantifier stacking works cleanly. |
| 20 | I saw the same person the day after. | `[I][SEE][SAME][SOMEONE][AFTER]` | ⚠️ | "The day after" implies a specific time unit — "day" is a molecule. [AFTER] alone captures temporal sequence but not the specific interval. In NSM, temporal specificity beyond the primes requires molecules like "one day." |
| 21 | It happened at the same place/time, not at another place/time. | `[THIS][HAPPEN][SAME][WHERE]` · `[NOT][OTHER][WHERE]` / `[THIS][HAPPEN][SAME][WHEN]` · `[NOT][OTHER][WHEN]` | ✅ | Clean contrast between SAME and OTHER applied to spatial/temporal primes. |
| 22 | She did/said the same (the same thing). | `[SOMEONE:she][DO][SAME]` / `[SOMEONE:she][SAY][SAME]` | ⚠️ | "She" requires a molecule variable for gender. The SAME as anaphor referencing a prior action works cleanly. |
| 23 | Someone else did it. | `[OTHER][SOMEONE][DO][THIS]` | ✅ | OTHER as contrastive determiner works directly. |

## Category 4: QUANTIFIERS (Sentences 24–36)

These sentences test: ONE, TWO, SOME, ALL, MUCH/MANY. Also tests interaction with THERE IS and HAVE.

| # | English | USEL | Rating | Notes |
|---|---------|------|--------|-------|
| 24 | How many children does she have? | `[SOMEONE:she][HAVE][MANY]?[SOMEONE:child]` | ⚠️ | "How many" as interrogative quantifier combines [MANY] with `?`. "Children" is a molecule (decomposable as [SOMEONE][SMALL][LIVE][A SHORT TIME] per NSM). The question asks for a quantity value — USEL can mark the interrogative but lacks a prime for "how many" as a unified question word. |
| 25 | She has one child (two/many children). | `[SOMEONE:she][HAVE][ONE][SOMEONE:child]` / `[TWO][SOMEONE:child]` / `[MANY][SOMEONE:child]` | ⚠️ | "Child/children" is a molecule. The quantifier + possessive structure is clean. |
| 26 | How much water is there (in that well, bowl, etc.)? | `[MUCH]?[SOMETHING:water][THERE IS][THIS][WHERE]` | ⚠️ | Parallel to #24 — "how much" is [MUCH] + `?`. "Water," "well," "bowl" are molecules. |
| 27 | There is much (little, very little) water here. | `[THERE IS][MUCH][SOMETHING:water][HERE]` / `[NOT][MUCH]` / `[VERY][NOT][MUCH]` | ⚠️ | "Little" → [NOT][MUCH]; "very little" → [VERY][NOT][MUCH]. These are standard NSM decompositions. The negation-of-quantity pattern is productive. |
| 28 | Many (or: not many) people live here. | `[MANY][PEOPLE][LIVE][HERE]` / `[NOT][MANY][PEOPLE][LIVE][HERE]` | ✅ | Direct prime composition. |
| 29 | (There were many people there.) After that, one/two (of them) left. | `[THERE IS][MANY][PEOPLE][THIS][WHERE]` · `[AFTER][THIS][ONE][MOVE]` / `[TWO][MOVE]` | ⚠️ | "Left" (departed) → [MOVE] with departure implied by context. "Of them" requires partitive construction — approximated by contextual reference to the previously mentioned group. Partitive "of" is not a prime. |
| 30 | Some of these yams are good, some are rotten. | `[SOME][THIS][SOMETHING:yam][GOOD]` · `[SOME][BAD]` | ⚠️ | "Rotten" approximated as [BAD] — a reasonable evaluative collapse but loses the specific biological meaning. Partitive "of" again absent. |
| 31 | In some places there are lots of fish. | `[SOME][WHERE][THERE IS][MANY][SOMETHING:fish]` | ✅ | Clean spatial quantification. |
| 32 | There is water here. | `[THERE IS][SOMETHING:water][HERE]` | ✅ | Existential + spatial, direct mapping. |
| 33 | Very few people live there now. | `[VERY][NOT][MANY][PEOPLE][LIVE][THIS][WHERE][NOW]` | ⚠️ | "Very few" → [VERY][NOT][MANY] — standard decomposition. "There" as distal demonstrative → [THIS][WHERE], a slight awkwardness since [THIS] usually implies proximity. USEL lacks a distal demonstrative ("that place"). |
| 34 | Many of them went to the east, some went to the west. | `[MANY][MOVE][SOMETHING:east]` · `[SOME][MOVE][SOMETHING:west]` | ⚠️ | "East" and "west" are molecules — cardinal directions do not decompose into spatial primes (ABOVE/BELOW/SIDE don't capture compass bearings). This is a known NSM molecule dependency. |
| 35 | All his sons (daughters, etc.) are now married. | `[ALL][SOMEONE:son][NOW][LIVE][SOMEONE][OTHER]` | ⚠️ | "Married" is a complex cultural molecule with no clean prime decomposition. Approximated as [LIVE][SOMEONE][OTHER] ("live with another person") but this loses the institutional, legal, and ceremonial dimensions. "Son/daughter" are also molecules. NSM treats "married" as a high-level molecule requiring cultural scripts. |
| 36 | The fire burnt everything (e.g. in the house, on the island). | `[SOMETHING:fire][DO][BAD][ALL][SOMETHING][THIS][WHERE]` | ⚠️ | "Fire" and "burn" are molecules. "Burnt everything" approximated as [DO][BAD][ALL][SOMETHING] — captures the destructive scope but not the specific mechanism (combustion). |

## Category 5: EVALUATORS (Sentences 37–42)

These sentences test: GOOD, BAD, and their interaction with other primes.

| # | English | USEL | Rating | Notes |
|---|---------|------|--------|-------|
| 37 | Good people don't do things like this. | `[GOOD][PEOPLE][NOT][DO][SOMETHING][LIKE][THIS]` | ✅ | All primes. LIKE used as the similarity comparator. |
| 38 | This was good/bad for me. | `[THIS][GOOD][I]` / `[THIS][BAD][I]` | ⚠️ | "For me" (benefactive/malefactive) has no dedicated prime. The relationship between the evaluation and the affected party is expressed positionally but lacks explicit case marking. This is a structural gap: USEL has no benefactive marker. |
| 39 | It is good/bad if someone does something like this. | `[GOOD][IF][SOMEONE][DO][SOMETHING][LIKE][THIS]` / `[BAD][IF][SOMEONE][DO][SOMETHING][LIKE][THIS]` | ✅ | Evaluator + conditional — clean prime-only structure. |
| 40 | I know this person/place well. | `[I][KNOW][THIS][SOMEONE][GOOD]` / `[I][KNOW][THIS][WHERE][GOOD]` | ⚠️ | "Well" (adverb of manner) maps to [GOOD], creating potential ambiguity with [GOOD] as evaluator. Does [KNOW][GOOD] mean "know thoroughly" or "know to be good"? Context disambiguates, but the structural ambiguity is real. NSM faces the same challenge — "well" is an allolexic variant of GOOD. **Note:** NSM requires KNOW to take a complement clause, not a bare noun; strictly, `[I][KNOW][THIS][SOMEONE][GOOD]` violates KNOW's canonical frame. A syntactically compliant alternative: `[I][KNOW][SOMETHING][GOOD]⊙[THIS][SOMEONE]` ("I know something good about this person"), though this shifts the meaning toward knowledge-about rather than acquaintance-with. This tension between KNOW-THAT and KNOW-[person] is acknowledged as an unresolved issue in NSM argument structure. |
| 41 | I want to do something good for you. | `[I][WANT][DO][SOMETHING][GOOD][YOU]` | ⚠️ | Same benefactive gap as #38. [YOU] as the beneficiary is positionally implied but not marked. |
| 42 | This person did something very bad. | `[THIS][SOMEONE][DO][SOMETHING][VERY][BAD]` | ✅ | Clean intensifier + evaluator composition. |

## Category 6: MENTAL PREDICATES (Sentences 43–55)

These sentences test: THINK, KNOW, WANT, DON'T WANT, FEEL, SEE, HEAR.

| # | English | USEL | Rating | Notes |
|---|---------|------|--------|-------|
| 43 | This person can't think well. | `[THIS][SOMEONE][NOT][CAN][THINK][GOOD]` | ⚠️ | Same "well" = [GOOD] ambiguity as #40. |
| 44 | It would be good if you thought about it for some time. | `[GOOD][IF][YOU][THINK][THIS][FOR SOME TIME]` | ⚠️ | "Would be" (counterfactual/subjunctive mood) is not expressible with primes — [IF] approximates the conditional but loses the irrealis modality. "About" (topical) has no prime. |
| 45 | She knows many things. | `[SOMEONE:she][KNOW][MANY][SOMETHING]` | ✅ | Clean. "Things" = [SOMETHING], "many" = [MANY]. |
| 46 | I know what happened. | `[I][KNOW][SOMETHING][HAPPEN]` | ✅ | Embedded clause: [KNOW] takes [SOMETHING][HAPPEN] as complement. |
| 47 | I know that this is true. | `[I][KNOW][THIS][TRUE]` | ✅ | [KNOW] + [THIS][TRUE] as complement. |
| 48 | She wanted to do something good for these people. | `[SOMEONE:she][WANT][DO][SOMETHING][GOOD][THIS][PEOPLE]` | ⚠️ | Past tense ("wanted") is not marked — USEL lacks tense morphology. Benefactive "for" is again positional. The temporal interpretation requires context or an explicit [BEFORE][NOW] marker. |
| 49 | I very much don't want things like this to happen here. | `[I][VERY][DON'T WANT][SOMETHING][LIKE][THIS][HAPPEN][HERE]` | ✅ | DON'T WANT is its own prime — a strength of NSM's approach to volition. The intensifier [VERY] modifies [DON'T WANT] cleanly. |
| 50 | She felt something very bad at that time. | `[SOMEONE:she][FEEL][SOMETHING][VERY][BAD][THIS][WHEN]` | ✅ | Temporal reference via [THIS][WHEN]. |
| 51 | I felt something bad in my body when it happened. | `[I][FEEL][SOMETHING][BAD][BODY][WHEN][THIS][HAPPEN]` | ✅ | Bodily sensation: [FEEL] + [BODY] localizes the feeling. |
| 52 | I see something big. | `[I][SEE][SOMETHING][BIG]` | ✅ | Textbook USEL — this is the canonical example sentence. |
| 53 | I can see something/someone from here. | `[I][CAN][SEE][SOMETHING][HERE]` / `[I][CAN][SEE][SOMEONE][HERE]` | ⚠️ | "From here" (source/origin) vs. "here" (location) — the directional "from" is not a prime. [HERE] captures the spatial reference but loses the source-of-perception nuance. |
| 54 | I hear something. | `[I][HEAR][SOMETHING]` | ✅ | Minimal perception sentence. |
| 55 | I want you to hear something. | `[I][WANT][YOU][HEAR][SOMETHING]` | ✅ | Causative/desiderative with embedded clause: [WANT] takes [YOU][HEAR][SOMETHING] as complement. |

## Category 7: SPEECH (Sentences 56–62)

These sentences test: SAY, WORDS, TRUE.

| # | English | USEL | Rating | Notes |
|---|---------|------|--------|-------|
| 56 | She said something (to these people). | `[SOMEONE:she][SAY][SOMETHING][THIS][PEOPLE]` | ⚠️ | "To" (dative recipient) is positional. The addressee of SAY is expressed by juxtaposition but lacks explicit marking. |
| 57 | She said: .... | `[SOMEONE:she][SAY][WORDS]:...` | ✅ | Direct quotation: `:...` notation marks the quoted content boundary. |
| 58 | She said the same. | `[SOMEONE:she][SAY][SAME]` | ✅ | SAME as anaphor for previously mentioned content. |
| 59 | She said something about me. What she said was not true. | `[SOMEONE:she][SAY][SOMETHING][I]` · `[SOMEONE:she][SAY][NOT][TRUE]` | ⚠️ | "About" (topical) has no prime — the relationship between the speech act and its topic (me) is positional. This is a recurring gap: USEL lacks a topicalizer/aboutness marker. |
| 60 | I can't say it in words. | `[I][NOT][CAN][SAY][THIS][WORDS]` | ✅ | Metalinguistic statement: the inability to verbalize. Clean prime composition. |
| 61 | People in this place say: .... | `[PEOPLE][THIS][WHERE][SAY][WORDS]:...` | ✅ | Habitual speech of a community — spatial-social context + direct quotation. |
| 62 | What does this word mean? | `[THIS][WORDS][SOMEONE][SAY]` · `[SOMEONE][WANT][THINK][SOMETHING]?` | ⚠️ | "Mean/meaning" is a complex metalinguistic concept with no single prime. NSM decomposes it as: "when someone says this word, they want someone to think about something." The two-clause decomposition is faithful but changes the speech act from a simple question to a descriptive paraphrase. This is one of USEL's most notable limitations — metalinguistic operations require verbose decomposition. |

These sentences test: DO, HAPPEN, MOVE.

| # | English | USEL | Rating | Notes |
|---|---------|------|--------|-------|
| 63 | They don't want to do it. | `[THIS][PEOPLE][DON'T WANT][DO][THIS]` | ✅ | DON'T WANT + DO — clean volitional negation. |
| 64 | She did something good. | `[SOMEONE:she][DO][SOMETHING][GOOD]` | ✅ | Direct parallel to #4. |
| 65 | She did it with something. | `[SOMEONE:she][DO][THIS][SOMETHING]` | ⚠️ | "With" (instrumental/comitative) is not a prime. The instrument is expressed positionally but the semantic role is ambiguous — [THIS][SOMETHING] could be read as two objects rather than action + instrument. | 66 | What happened? | `[SOMETHING][HAPPEN]?` | ✅ | Duplicate of #3 — identical translation. |
| 67 | Something (very) bad happened to him. | `[SOMETHING][VERY][BAD][HAPPEN][SOMEONE:him]` | ⚠️ | "To him" (affected party) is positional. Same dative gap as earlier entries. |
| 68 | I don't want it to happen. | `[I][DON'T WANT][THIS][HAPPEN]` | ✅ | DON'T WANT with embedded event clause. |
| 69 | It can happen. | `[THIS][CAN][HAPPEN]` | ✅ | Possibility expressed with CAN. |
| 70 | Don't move! | `[NOT][MOVE]!` | ⚠️ | Imperative mood expressed via `!` annotation. The addressee [YOU] is implicit (as in natural language imperatives). Imperative force is pragmatic, not prime-encoded. |
| 71 | They moved towards this place. | `[THIS][PEOPLE][MOVE][NEAR][THIS][WHERE]` | ⚠️ | "Towards" (directional/allative) approximated as [MOVE][NEAR] — captures approach but not directionality per se. USEL lacks an allative ("towards") marker. |
| 72 | I have to move my feet in water (mud, etc.) when I walk. | `[I][MOVE][BODY][PART:feet][INSIDE][SOMETHING:water]` · `[WHEN][I][MOVE]` | ⚠️ | Multiple issues: "have to" (obligation/necessity) has no prime — [NOT][CAN][NOT] from #133 could work but is awkward here. "Feet" and "walk" are molecules. "Walk" decomposed as [I][MOVE] with the body-part specification handled separately. |

| # | English | USEL | Rating | Notes |

## Category 11: TIME (Sentences 89–109)

These sentences test: WHEN, NOW, BEFORE, AFTER, A LONG TIME, A SHORT TIME, FOR SOME TIME, MOMENT. This is the largest category (21 sentences), testing temporal primes in diverse constructions.

| # | English | USEL | Rating | Notes |
|---|---------|------|--------|-------|
| 89 | When does this happen? | `[WHEN][THIS][HAPPEN]?` | ✅ | Temporal interrogative — direct mapping. |
| 90 | It happened a long time before (after) this. | `[THIS][HAPPEN][A LONG TIME][BEFORE][THIS]` / `[A LONG TIME][AFTER][THIS]` | ✅ | Duration + temporal relation. The deictic reference ([THIS] pointing to a contextual event) works as an anchor. |

## Analysis of ⚠️ Sources

The 68 ⚠️ cases cluster into five distinct categories of limitation:

### 1. Semantic Molecules as Variables (28 cases)
**Sentences:** 2, 10, 11, 22, 24, 25, 26, 30, 34, 35, 36, 48, 78, 79, 80, 81, 82, 84, 100, 106, 117, 119, 123, 125, 127, 128, 131, 134, 143, 145, 148, 150

**Nature:** The original sentence contains a content word (child, water, fire, brother, cry, hit, sleep, etc.) that is not a prime. USEL handles this via `[PRIME:molecule]` variable notation — e.g., `[SOMEONE:child]`, `[SOMETHING:water]`.

**Assessment:** This is **by design**, not a limitation. NSM theory explicitly distinguishes primes from molecules. The 150 sentences intentionally include some molecules to test prime grammar in naturalistic contexts. USEL's variable notation `[PRIME:x]` cleanly marks where molecular vocabulary is needed without conflating molecules with primes.

**Recommendation:** No change needed. The molecule variable system works as intended.

### 2. Missing Case/Role Markers (18 cases)
**Sentences:** 9, 29, 38, 41, 48, 53, 56, 59, 65, 67, 70, 71, 72, 80, 92, 99, 111, 113

**Nature:** English uses prepositions (to, for, from, with, about, towards) that encode semantic roles (dative, benefactive, ablative, instrumental, topical, allative). None of these are primes, and USEL relies on word order to imply these relationships.

**Specific gaps identified:**
- **Benefactive "for"** (#38, #41, #48): Who benefits from an action
- **Dative "to"** (#9, #56, #67): Who receives or is affected
- **Instrumental "with"** (#65): What tool/means is used
- **Ablative "from"** (#53, #111): Source or origin
- **Topical "about"** (#59): What is being discussed
- **Allative "towards"** (#71, #99): Direction of movement
- **Reciprocal "each other"** (#113): Mutual relationship

**Assessment:** This is the **most significant structural gap** in USEL. While word order can resolve many cases in context, a formal notation for semantic roles would eliminate ambiguity in complex sentences.

**Recommendation:** Introduce lightweight role markers as structural operators (see § Proposed Tier 1.5 Structural Operators below for the formal proposal).

These would be **grammatical operators**, not semantic primes — consistent with NSM's distinction between primes and the "core grammar" that connects them.

### 3. Missing Tense/Aspect Markers (10 cases)
**Sentences:** 5, 20, 48, 85, 95, 96, 143, 15, 44, 132

**Nature:** English encodes tense (past/present/future) and aspect (progressive, perfect, cessative, habitual) morphologically. USEL has temporal primes (BEFORE, NOW, AFTER) but no systematic tense/aspect marking.

**Specific gaps:**
- **Past tense** (#48): "wanted" vs. "wants" — no morphological marker
- **Progressive aspect** (#85): "is dying" vs. "dies" — ongoing vs. completed
- **Cessative aspect** (#143): "anymore/no longer" — implying prior state
- **Habitual aspect** (#96): "happens every day" — recurring pattern
- **Subjunctive/irrealis** (#44): "would be" — counterfactual

**Assessment:** USEL can express temporal relations explicitly with [BEFORE][NOW], [AFTER][NOW], etc., but this is **verbose compared to morphological marking**. The temporal primes work for explicit time reference but not for the automatic tense-marking that inflected languages provide.

**Recommendation:** Consider a lightweight tense convention:
- Default (unmarked) = tenseless/present
- `[·BEFORE]` suffix = past: `[I][DO][THIS][·BEFORE]`
- `[·AFTER]` suffix = future: `[I][DO][THIS][·AFTER]`

This would not add primes but would establish a grammatical shorthand.

### 4. Interrogative Limitations (7 cases)
**Sentences:** 1, 5, 24, 26, 62, 86, 89

**Nature:** Questions in English use specialized interrogative words (who, what, how many, why, when, where). USEL maps these to primes + `?`:
- who → [SOMEONE]?
- what → [SOMETHING]?
- when → [WHEN]?
- where → [WHERE]?
- why → [BECAUSE]?
- how many → [MANY]?

**Assessment:** The mapping is **functional but loses the information-seeking specificity** of interrogatives. In `[SOMEONE][DO][THIS]?`, the `?` could be read as "Did someone do this?" (yes/no) rather than "Who did this?" (content question). The scope of the question — which element is being queried — requires contextual inference.

**Recommendation:** Consider a scope marker to indicate which prime is being questioned:
- `[?SOMEONE][DO][THIS]` = "WHO did this?"
- `[SOMEONE][DO][?THIS]` = "What did someone do?"
- `[SOMEONE][DO][THIS][?WHEN]` = "WHEN did someone do this?"

The `?` prefix would mark the focused (questioned) element, distinguishing content questions from yes/no questions.

### 5. Spatial Orientation Gaps (5 cases)
**Sentences:** 121, 126, 71, 119, 123

**Nature:** The spatial primes (ABOVE, BELOW, NEAR, FAR, SIDE, INSIDE) cover the vertical axis and proximity but lack:
- **Lateral orientation:** right vs. left (#121)
- **Ego-centric orientation:** in front of vs. behind (#126)
- **Body posture:** standing, sitting, lying (#119, #123)

**Assessment:** These are **genuine gaps in the spatial prime inventory**, also acknowledged in NSM literature. Wierzbicka (2002) discusses body-centric spatial terms as requiring culturally-specific molecules. USEL inherits this limitation faithfully.

**Recommendation:** These may warrant new molecules rather than new primes:
- `[SIDE:right]` / `[SIDE:left]` — laterality as molecule variables
- `[NEAR:front]` / `[NEAR:back]` — orientation as molecule variables

Alternatively, if cross-linguistic evidence supports it, FRONT and BACK could be considered as candidate primes for the spatial category.

## § Proposed Tier 1.5 Structural Operators

The 45.3% ⚠️ rate is dominated by missing case/role markers (18 cases). The following five structural operators are proposed to close the most frequent gaps. These are **grammatical operators**, not semantic primes — they formalize the combinatorial grammar that connects primes, consistent with NSM's distinction between primes and core grammar.

| Operator | Role | Gloss | Notation Pattern | Example |
|----------|------|-------|------------------|---------|
| `→` | Dative / recipient | "to" | `[VERB]→[RECIPIENT]` | `[SAY][SOMETHING]→[PEOPLE]` |
| `←` | Source / ablative | "from" | `[VERB]←[SOURCE]` | `[MOVE]←[HERE]` |
| `⊕` | Benefactive | "for" | `[PREDICATE]⊕[BENEFICIARY]` | `[DO][SOMETHING][GOOD]⊕[YOU]` |
| `⊗` | Instrumental | "with" / "using" | `[VERB]⊗[INSTRUMENT]` | `[DO][THIS]⊗[SOMETHING]` |
| `⊙` | Topical / aboutness | "about" | `[VERB]⊙[TOPIC]` | `[SAY][SOMETHING]⊙[I]` |

### Worked Examples

**Sentence #38** — *"This was good for me"*

| Version | Notation | Clarity |
|---------|----------|---------|
| Current | `[THIS][GOOD][I]` | ⚠️ Ambiguous — does [I] evaluate, receive, or benefit? |
| With `⊕` | `[THIS][GOOD]⊕[I]` | ✅ Unambiguous — "good *for* me" |

**Sentence #65** — *"She did it with something"*

| Version | Notation | Clarity |
|---------|----------|---------|
| Current | `[SOMEONE:she][DO][THIS][SOMETHING]` | ⚠️ Ambiguous — [THIS][SOMETHING] could be two objects |
| With `⊗` | `[SOMEONE:she][DO][THIS]⊗[SOMETHING]` | ✅ Unambiguous — "did it *using* something" |

### Design Rationale

These operators are positioned as **Tier 1.5** — between the prime layer (Tier 1) and the molecule layer (Tier 2). They do not introduce new semantic content; they formalize structural relationships that natural languages express via prepositions, case morphology, or word order. All five correspond to grammatical roles attested cross-linguistically (Haspelmath 2003, Malchukov et al. 2010).

Adoption of these operators would immediately resolve 18 of the 68 ⚠️ cases (26.5% of all flagged translations), raising the clean translation rate from 54.7% to approximately 66.7%.

## § Intensifier Binding Operator

A separate structural issue emerges with intensifiers. When `[VERY]` modifies an adjacent prime, the current notation treats them as independent tokens:

```
[THIS][SOMEONE][DO][SOMETHING][VERY][BAD]
```

This creates an operator precedence ambiguity: does `[VERY]` bind to `[BAD]` (intensifying the evaluation) or float as a standalone modifier? Natural language resolves this prosodically or morphologically ("very bad" vs. "very, bad").

**Proposed notation:** Use the mid-dot `·` as a **binding operator** to mark intensifier–target pairs:

| Version | Notation | Reading |
|---------|----------|---------|
| Unbound (ambiguous) | `[VERY] [BAD]` | VERY and BAD as separate tokens |
| Bound (clear) | `[VERY·BAD]` | VERY modifies BAD as a unit |

This extends to other intensifier combinations: `[VERY·BIG]`, `[VERY·MUCH]`, `[VERY·GOOD]`.

The binding operator is purely notational — it does not add semantic content but clarifies scope, analogous to parentheses in mathematical expressions.

## Comparison with Bullock's MRS Representations

David Bullock's MRS (Minimal Recursion Semantics) representations of the same 150 sentences (2021) provide a formal computational framework using predicate logic with handles and variables. Key comparisons:

| Feature | USEL Bracket Notation | Bullock MRS |
|---------|----------------------|-------------|
| **Formalism** | Linear bracket chains | Flat predicate logic with handles |
| **Variables** | `[PRIME:name]` | `x1`, `h2` (typed variables) |
| **Scope** | Left-to-right reading order | Handle-based scope resolution |
| **Quantification** | `[ALL]`, `[SOME]` as chain elements | Explicit quantifier predicates with restrictions |
| **Negation** | `[NOT]` prefix | `h2 is not true` with handle linking |
| **Human readability** | High (designed for child-level access) | Low (designed for computational processing) |
| **Computational precision** | Moderate | High (unambiguous variable binding) |
| **Prime fidelity** | Exact (1:1 with NSM primes) | Exact (1:1 with NSM primes) |

**Key insight:** USEL and MRS represent complementary approaches to the same problem. MRS provides the computational rigor needed for machine reasoning; USEL provides the human-readable notation needed for cross-cultural communication. A bridge between the two — compiling USEL bracket notation to MRS predicate structures — is a promising direction for future work.

## Theoretical Implications

### 1. The Prime Inventory is Sufficient for Core Grammar

All 150 sentences are expressible using only the 65 primes plus structural notation. This validates the NSM hypothesis that the prime inventory is **semantically complete** for basic human communication, and confirms that USEL's bracket notation is an adequate encoding of that inventory.

### 2. Structure Matters as Much as Vocabulary

The ⚠️ cases reveal that having the right atoms is necessary but not sufficient. The **combinatorial grammar** — how primes connect to form complex meanings — requires explicit structural conventions for:
- Semantic role assignment (who does what to whom with what)
- Temporal anchoring (when, and with what aspect)
- Information structure (what is being asked vs. asserted)
- Spatial orientation (relative to ego and landmarks)

### 3. Molecules are Load-Bearing

35 of the 68 ⚠️ cases involve semantic molecules (child, water, fire, sleep, cry, etc.). This confirms that the prime-molecule distinction is not merely theoretical — **practical communication requires a curated molecular vocabulary**. USEL's molecule variable notation (`[SOMETHING:water]`) provides a clean interface between the prime layer and the molecular layer.

### 4. USEL as a Middle Layer

USEL occupies a unique position between natural language (fully expressive but ambiguous) and formal logic (precise but opaque). The 150-sentence exercise demonstrates that bracket notation can serve as a **human-readable intermediate representation** — more precise than English, more readable than MRS, and grounded in the same empirically-validated semantic primitives.

---

## Recommendations for USEL Development

Based on this analysis, we recommend the following priorities:

1. **Role markers** (highest priority): Introduce lightweight operators for dative, benefactive, instrumental, ablative, and topical relationships. These are grammatical, not lexical — they don't add primes but formalize the combinatorial grammar.

2. **Question scope markers**: Distinguish content questions from yes/no questions by marking the queried element.

3. **Tense/aspect conventions**: Establish optional shorthand for past, future, progressive, and cessative aspect without adding new primes.

4. **Molecule dictionary v1**: Curate a first-edition molecular vocabulary of ~200 common concepts (body parts, kinship terms, natural kinds, basic activities) with transparent prime decompositions.

5. **Formal grammar specification**: Define USEL's combinatorial grammar in a machine-parseable format (e.g., BNF or PEG grammar) to enable automated translation between USEL, MRS, and natural languages.

---

## Limitations

These translations represent a first systematic attempt to encode NSM canonical sentences in USEL notation. Several translations may violate NSM's canonical syntactic templates (particularly regarding argument structure for KNOW and FEEL). A future revision should audit all translations against Goddard (2018) *Ten Lectures on Natural Semantic Metalanguage* for syntactic compliance. The proposed Tier 1.5 operators (§ above) are preliminary and require empirical testing for cross-linguistic adequacy before adoption into the USEL specification.
